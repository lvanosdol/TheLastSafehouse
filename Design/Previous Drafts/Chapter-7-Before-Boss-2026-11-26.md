## Lesson 1: Digital Literacy and Evaluating Information

**Depth:** Detailed.
**Workspace:** Split-screen Learn & Try.

### Story opening

Finch's temporary hold has stopped the purge for now. As he prepares a report for the people inside the Habitats, he finds several versions of the recovered records: original log entries, Mara's explanations, and an automatically generated summary.

> **Finch:** “We have evidence. We also have several descriptions of what the evidence supposedly means.”
>
> **Player:** “Are they different?”
>
> **Finch:** “In some important places. Before we share anything, we should check what the sources actually say.”

Finch opens the **Information Review Board**, displaying authorized source records beside claims and summaries. The protected evidence store remains sealed; the board uses recovered copies already available to Finch.

### Learn & Try pages

The lesson stays on the left while the review board appears on the right. The player can inspect each example and revisit the pages during the checkpoint.

#### Page 1: Digital literacy

Digital literacy includes finding, understanding, evaluating, and using digital information responsibly. A polished presentation or confident answer does not guarantee accuracy.

The player compares two fictional messages and identifies what they would need to know before trusting either.

#### Page 2: Identify the source

Ask who created a record, when, what it was intended to show, and whether it is an original or a copy. A source may be useful for one question but not another. Missing information about its origin should be noted rather than assumed.

The player inspects a log entry's source, timestamp, and copy status.

#### Page 3: Facts, claims, and uncertainty

An observation records what the available evidence shows. A claim is an explanation or assertion that may need further support. An unanswered question should remain unanswered until there is enough evidence.

The player sorts statements into **supported**, **not established**, and **needs more information**, with feedback explaining each choice.

#### Page 4: Check context

An excerpt can omit surrounding information, and records can appear to disagree because they describe different times or events. Compare dates, nearby entries, and the original source before drawing a conclusion.

The player opens an excerpt beside its surrounding entries and identifies what was omitted.

#### Page 5: Integrity is not the same as truth

A matching checksum can show that a copy matches the expected data. A verified signature can help establish that a record was signed with a trusted key and has not changed since signing. Neither proves the author was correct or the claims are true.

The player separates what a verification result establishes from what still needs independent evidence.

#### Page 6: Evaluate AI-generated summaries

An AI summary can help organize information, but it can omit context, confuse sources, or state an unsupported interpretation as fact. Compare important statements with the underlying records. Keep originals separate from summaries and label generated or reconstructed content clearly.

The player marks an unsupported sentence for revision.

#### Page 7: Recognize misleading presentation

Selective excerpts, missing dates, altered images, or emotionally charged wording can mislead. Not every mistake is intentional; check the source and context before repeating a claim.

The player examines a fictional excerpt whose surrounding record changes its meaning.

#### Page 8: Share only what is needed

Before sharing a record, consider who needs it, what they need to understand, and whether restricted details should be withheld or redacted. Preserve the original separately; do not silently alter it.

The player creates a limited test excerpt for an authorized audience.

### Checkpoint: Review the evidence summary

#### Guided stage

Finch shows the player a preserved change record:

```text
AUDIT EXPORT SERVICE: DISABLED
CHANGE SOURCE: M.VALE
LABEL: TEMPORARY MAINTENANCE
```

A sample summary says: “Mara personally disabled the service to conceal evidence.”

The player identifies that the record names the **M.VALE account** as the change source, but this entry alone does not establish who used it or why. Finch helps revise the sentence without erasing the original record or presenting Mara's explanation as verified.

#### Main task

The player reviews a simulated evidence summary and its linked sources:

1. Inspect each source's origin, time, and whether it is an original or a copy.
2. Compare the summary with full source entries and their surrounding context.
3. Mark statements as **supported**, **not established**, or **needs more information**, considering all relevant sources.
4. Identify an AI-generated statement that goes beyond its sources and replace it with a supported description or explicit uncertainty.
5. Identify what a checksum or signature result does—and does not—verify.
6. Prepare a limited excerpt for an authorized audience without exposing unrelated restricted information. Preserve the source and label the generated summary separately.

The board gives specific feedback and allows revision. It does not ask the player to decide that all claims are true simply because integrity checks passed.

### Feedback and hints

Three hints point toward checking the original source, separating what a record says from an explanation, and reviewing the intended audience. They do not reveal the whole solution. Hints and retries are unlimited and penalty-free.

### Completion and story result

Finch keeps the reviewed summary alongside—not in place of—the preserved sources. Unsupported statements remain marked as unverified, and original records remain unchanged.

> **Finch:** “The evidence is strong enough to matter. That is all the more reason to say exactly what it shows.”

The reviewed material can support later communication and rescue decisions. Finch's temporary hold remains active; the inhabitants and their restricted records are not exposed by completing the lesson.

Completed activity progress saves automatically. An unfinished checkpoint stage restarts if the player leaves.

### Accessibility

Source cards, comparison views, classifications, and redaction controls work with keyboard and screen reader. Each claim can be reached with its linked source and context. Labels and feedback use text rather than color alone, and the player can review information without a time limit.

## Lesson 2: Technical Communication for Different Audiences

**Depth:** Detailed.
**Workspace:** Split-screen Learn & Try.

### Story opening

Finch's temporary hold is keeping the purge from proceeding, but the inhabitants need to know what is happening. The people maintaining the core also need enough technical detail to keep the hold stable.

> **Finch:** “The same situation needs two explanations.”
>
> **Player:** “One for the people inside, and one for whoever can help us keep the doors safe.”
>
> **Finch:** “Exactly. Neither needs exaggeration. Both need the truth.”

Finch opens the **Communication Desk**. It contains a simulated incident summary, reviewed evidence from Lesson 1, audience cards, and message drafts. Any urgent safety notice can be sent immediately; the exercise does not require the player to complete a report before warning someone in danger. All messages and recipients are fictional.

### Learn & Try pages

The lesson stays on the left while the Communication Desk appears on the right. The player can review pages during the checkpoint.

#### Page 1: Start with the audience and purpose

Different people need different information to act. A technical teammate may need the affected system, status, and relevant checks. An inhabitant may first need to know whether a door is safe, what is being done, and how to ask for help. Neither audience needs unrelated private details.

The player compares two fictional recipients and selects the information each needs first.

#### Page 2: Be clear and specific

Use direct language, explain unfamiliar terms, and include the action or decision needed. Avoid unexplained abbreviations and vague instructions such as “fix it” or “everything is fine.”

The player revises an unclear message into a short, actionable update.

#### Page 3: Report the situation without exaggeration

Describe what is happening, its impact, and the urgency supported by the evidence. A temporary hold is not a permanent fix. Avoid promising that everyone is safe when that has not been verified.

The player compares two draft alerts and identifies an unsupported reassurance.

#### Page 4: Separate facts, uncertainty, and recommendations

Clearly distinguish what has been observed, what is still unknown, and what action is recommended. An explanation from Mara, an account name in a log, and a verified technical status do not all carry the same meaning.

The player labels statements in a sample update and links factual claims to their sources.

#### Page 5: Give a technical handoff

A useful handoff can include the affected system, current status, relevant symptoms, checks already completed, changes made, and the next requested action. Include timestamps or references where available. Do not send passwords, unrestricted evidence, or unnecessary personal information.

The player assembles a simulated handoff for an authorized recovery teammate.

#### Page 6: Write for a nontechnical audience

Explain the immediate situation in everyday language. State what people can do, how they can ask for help, and when they can expect another update. If the answer is not yet known, say so. People affected should have an opportunity to ask questions and express their preferences.

The player rewrites a technical status message for a Habitat occupant without changing its meaning.

#### Page 7: Choose a safe channel and protect privacy

Use an approved channel for the intended audience. Share the minimum relevant information, and keep source records separate from any redacted copy or summary. A recipient's need for a safety update does not automatically authorize access to private records.

The player selects fictional internal channels and previews what each audience can see.

#### Page 8: Confirm understanding and follow up

A message being sent does not mean it was received or understood. Where possible, check delivery, invite questions, record the response, and provide updates when the situation changes. A lack of response should remain visible rather than being treated as consent.

The player reviews a simulated delivery receipt and a recipient's question, then chooses a clear follow-up.

### Checkpoint: Communicate the containment situation

#### Guided stage

Finch shows the player a draft:

> “Everything is safe. The purge is fixed.”

The status panel shows that the purge is **temporarily held**, not permanently stopped. The player changes the message to accurately describe the hold and identifies that the final resolution is still pending.

#### Main task

The player prepares two simulated communications using the reviewed source material:

1. **Technical handoff:** State that the containment request is temporarily held, identify the affected core and relevant status, summarize completed checks, and request help maintaining the hold. Mark unverified details as unknown and link relevant source records.
2. **Habitat update:** Explain the temporary hold in plain language, say that the team is working to open doors safely, and provide a way to ask for help or share preferences. Do not promise that the purge has been permanently stopped.
3. Select an approved fictional channel for each audience. Exclude credentials and unrelated restricted records, keeping the original evidence unchanged.
4. Review the messages, send them within the simulation, and check their delivery status. A simulated inhabitant asks a question; the player responds accurately, acknowledging any information still unknown.

The desk accepts clear equivalent wording rather than requiring one exact sentence. If a simulated message is undelivered, the player receives a visible status and can select an appropriate available channel or escalate; silence is not treated as consent.

### Feedback and hints

The desk highlights unsupported claims, unexplained technical terms for a nontechnical audience, unnecessary private details, and missing actions or follow-up. The player can revise each draft.

Three hints focus on the recipient's needs, the difference between a temporary hold and a permanent fix, and what information is necessary to share. They do not reveal the whole solution. Hints and retries are unlimited and penalty-free.

### Completion and story result

The authorized recovery teammate receives the technical handoff. The Habitat update is delivered, and Finch records the simulated response and any remaining questions.

> **Inhabitant:** “You're really going to tell us what happens next?”
>
> **Player:** “What we know, what we don't, and what choices you have.”
>
> **Finch:** “And when the answer changes, we'll tell you that too.”

Finch preserves the messages separately from the evidence sources. The temporary hold remains active; sending an update does not open the Habitat or give anyone unrestricted control.

Completed activity progress saves automatically. An unfinished checkpoint stage restarts if the player leaves.

### Accessibility

Audience cards, message fields, previews, delivery statuses, and response controls work with keyboard and screen reader. Messages can be reviewed and edited before sending. Status and feedback use text rather than color alone, and there is no lesson time limit.

## Lesson 3: Collaboration, Escalation, and Responsibility

**Depth:** Detailed.
**Workspace:** Split-screen Learn & Try.

### Story opening

The Habitat has received Finch's update, and the recovery teammate has the technical handoff. But keeping the temporary hold stable, protecting the evidence, and planning a safe release require different responsibilities.

> **Finch:** “We have people who can help. We need to be clear about what each of us is doing.”
>
> **Player:** “And the people inside get a say in what happens to them.”
>
> **Finch:** “Yes. A rescue should not become another way to take control away.”

Finch opens the **Coordination Board**. It shows simulated roles, requests, current tasks, evidence-sharing controls, and a decision log. The temporary purge hold remains active; the board does not open doors or change real systems.

### Learn & Try pages

The lesson stays on the left while the board appears on the right. The player can review pages during the checkpoint.

#### Page 1: Work together with clear roles

A role describes a responsibility, not unlimited authority. Clear assignments help prevent essential tasks from being missed or duplicated. People may contribute to more than one task when appropriate.

The player matches fictional roles to tasks such as monitoring the core, communicating with inhabitants, and preserving evidence.

#### Page 2: Include the people affected

Inhabitants can have different needs and preferences. Give them accessible information and a way to ask questions or express choices. A person's silence does not mean they consent, and one person should not automatically speak for everyone.

The player reviews two simulated responses and records them separately without assuming they represent all inhabitants.

#### Page 3: Share information responsibly

Give each recipient the information needed for their role. An authorized technician may need system status without receiving private subject records. Preserve original evidence separately from redacted copies or summaries.

The player previews what a fictional technician and an inhabitant can see, then removes unrelated restricted details from each message.

#### Page 4: Know when to escalate

Escalation means seeking help or a decision from an appropriate person or team when a problem exceeds your authority, expertise, or available information. Escalating is not abandoning the task: continue the safe work you are authorized and able to do.

The player chooses who should handle a simulated life-support warning and keeps monitoring the temporary hold while waiting for a response.

#### Page 5: Challenge an unsafe instruction

When an instruction risks people, privacy, or evidence, pause the unsafe action, explain the concern, and seek an appropriate review. An instruction can come from someone with authority and still need to be challenged. In this story, Mara's facilities authority permits her containment request; the player cannot assume a permissions check will block it.

The player responds to a fictional request to delete an evidence copy while continuing a separate authorized safety task.

#### Page 6: Record decisions and disagreements

A useful decision log records the question, available evidence, responsible role, action, result, and any unresolved disagreement. Record what someone said separately from what has been verified. Documentation should not expose private details unnecessarily.

The player records a decision and a differing view without editing the original evidence.

#### Page 7: Confirm handoffs and follow up

Assigning a task is not the same as confirming it was accepted or completed. Track who has acknowledged a request, what remains pending, and when to follow up. If no one responds, escalate using an available appropriate channel rather than assuming success.

The player reviews a simulated handoff with one acknowledged task and one pending task, then chooses a follow-up.

### Checkpoint: Coordinate the response

#### Guided stage

Finch shows a fictional request to erase an evidence copy to simplify a repair. The player identifies the risk, declines the destructive action, and selects an appropriate review path. Finch explains that the team can continue other safe recovery work while the question is escalated.

#### Main task

The player uses the Coordination Board to:

1. Assign monitoring of the temporary hold to the authorized recovery role and confirm the task is acknowledged.
2. Arrange an accessible channel for Habitat inhabitants to ask questions and share individual preferences. Record responses separately; an unanswered request is not consent.
3. Share a limited technical status with the recovery teammate and preserve the original evidence and any redacted copy separately.
4. Escalate a simulated life-support warning to an appropriate qualified responder while continuing authorized monitoring. The warning remains visibly **pending** until the responder acknowledges and reports a result.
5. Respond to an unsafe instruction to remove the evidence copy by explaining the concern and requesting review, rather than deleting it or assuming Mara's authority will be denied automatically.
6. Record the assignments, acknowledgements, evidence used, disagreement, and unresolved items in the decision log.

The board accepts equivalent safe assignments where responsibilities and authority are clear. It does not require the player to invent an inhabitant's preference or treat an unacknowledged handoff as complete.

### Feedback and hints

The board identifies unassigned work, information shared beyond a recipient's needs, pending handoffs, and unsupported assumptions about consent or authority. The player can revise choices.

Three hints point toward matching responsibilities, checking acknowledgements, and continuing safe work while escalating. They do not reveal the whole solution. Hints and retries are unlimited and penalty-free.

### Completion and story result

The recovery role confirms monitoring of the hold. A qualified fictional responder acknowledges the life-support warning and reports that the **simulated check** is complete. Finch keeps the outcome and the inhabitants' individual responses in the decision log.

> **Inhabitant:** “You're asking what we want?”
>
> **Player:** “Yes. We're working to make it safe, and we won't decide everything for you.”
>
> **Finch:** “There are still questions to answer, but at least they will be your questions too.”

The temporary hold remains active. Completing the lesson does not open the Habitats, resolve the purge, or grant anyone unrestricted control.

Completed activity progress saves automatically. An unfinished checkpoint stage restarts if the player leaves.

### Accessibility

Role cards, assignments, evidence previews, acknowledgement states, and the decision log work with keyboard and screen reader. Pending and completed tasks use text labels, not color alone. Inhabitant communications provide accessible ways to respond, and the lesson has no time limit.

## Lesson 4: Capstone Decision-Making

**Depth:** Detailed.
**Workspace:** Split-screen Learn & Try.

### Story opening

Finch's temporary hold is still active. The recovery team has acknowledged its assignments, and Habitat inhabitants have a channel for questions and preferences. Before anyone attempts to open the doors, the team needs a safe plan that brings the skills from the whole game together.

> **Finch:** “We have repaired systems before. This time, the people depending on them get a voice in the plan.”
>
> **Player:** “Then we check the systems, listen to them, and make sure no one can take control of everything again.”
>
> **Finch:** “A sensible plan. Let's test it before we put anyone at risk.”

Finch opens the **Recovery Planning Table**. The player uses a simulated copy of the facility's systems to review a proposed release plan. Testing does not operate the real Habitat doors or alter the preserved evidence. The protected evidence store remains sealed.

### Learn & Try pages

The lesson stays on the left while the planning table appears on the right. The player can review pages during the checkpoint.

#### Page 1: Start with people and the immediate risk

Identify who could be affected, what is urgent, and what is not yet known. A temporary hold provides time to plan, but it does not resolve the purge. Ask inhabitants what they need and preserve different responses separately; silence is not consent.

The player reviews the current safety update and two fictional inhabitant responses.

#### Page 2: Check the systems before acting

A status indicator is a useful starting point, not always the full picture. Review the Habitat's life-support status, recent alerts, and available independent checks. Escalate a warning to a qualified responder rather than assuming a green indicator means every condition is safe.

The player compares a current status panel with a recent alert and a responder's simulated check.

#### Page 3: Use a troubleshooting method

When a system reports a problem, identify the symptom, gather evidence, form a theory, test one change at a time, and verify the result. Document changes and avoid disturbing original records.

The player reviews a fictional communications fault and chooses a safe next test instead of changing several settings at once.

#### Page 4: Check network and communications

A working local connection does not guarantee that a remote service is available. Check the relevant connection, approved route, and actual service. A timeout alone does not establish why a test failed.

The player checks a simulated Habitat communication channel and confirms that a test message is received and acknowledged.

#### Page 5: Find the right records

A database can help locate the status and history needed for a decision. Select only relevant fields and use authorized, read-only access when changing data is unnecessary. A record's account name identifies the account recorded, not necessarily the person who used it.

The player filters a fictional status table by Habitat and compares the result with a separate current status check.

#### Page 6: Review a proposed automation rule

A rule tells a system what to do when specified conditions are met. Conditions must reflect the intended safety checks, and an automatic action should not replace required human review or the inhabitants' choices.

The player inspects a simple simulated rule that requests a release review only after the hold, life-support check, and communications check are confirmed. The rule does **not** open a door automatically.

#### Page 7: Preserve backups and evidence

Verify that recoverable copies are available before making a risky change. Keep originals separate from working copies and AI-generated interpretations. A restore test checks recoverability; it does not prove that the contents are true.

The player identifies the protected original, an authorized working copy, and a verified backup destination.

#### Page 8: Limit authority and plan a handoff

Give each role the access needed for its task, not unrestricted control. Important decisions may require independent review or more than one authorized participant. Document the plan, identify who will carry out each step, and include how inhabitants can communicate their choices.

The player reviews a proposed access plan and flags a role that has unnecessary control over both evidence and Habitat doors.

### Checkpoint: Build a safe release plan

#### Guided stage

Finch shows a proposed rule:

```text
IF PURGE HOLD IS ACTIVE
THEN OPEN HABITAT DOOR
```

The player identifies that the hold alone does not establish whether life support, communications, or the release plan is ready. Finch helps change the simulated rule so it **requests a review** after the listed checks, rather than automatically opening the door.

#### Main task

The player works through a simulated plan for Habitat 02:

1. Review the current hold status, the inhabitant messages, and the decision log. Record known facts, open questions, and individual preferences without assuming a missing response means consent.
2. Check the simulated life-support status and recent alert. Confirm the qualified responder's check or escalate an unresolved warning before marking that part of the plan ready.
3. Test the Habitat communication channel and confirm a simulated message is acknowledged. Investigate a failure using one change at a time.
4. Use the authorized read-only status view to locate relevant Habitat records, then compare them with the current checks. Do not edit the source records.
5. Confirm that the evidence originals remain preserved and a separate backup has a successful restore test.
6. Review the release-review rule and the role permissions. The rule requests a review rather than opening a door, and no single role receives unrestricted control over both the evidence and the Habitat.
7. Document the proposed sequence, assigned responsibilities, unresolved questions, and how inhabitants can communicate their choices. Mark the plan **ready for final review**, not executed.

The planning table accepts equivalent safe plans when the required checks, responsibilities, evidence protections, and inhabitant input are accounted for. A pending check remains pending; the simulation does not invent an acknowledgement or preference.

### Feedback and hints

The table explains which requirement is incomplete and allows the player to revise the plan. It distinguishes a simulated test from an actual facility action.

Three hints point toward checking the people and systems involved, preserving evidence before changes, and separating a review request from an automatic door action. They do not reveal the whole solution. Hints and retries are unlimited and penalty-free.

### Completion and story result

The team has a reviewed plan, but the doors remain sealed and Mara's purge has not been permanently stopped. The final boss will put the plan to use and resolve the facility-wide threat.

> **Finch:** “The plan is ready. The people inside know what we're trying to do.”
>
> **Player:** “Then we do it together.”

The temporary hold remains active. Completed activity progress saves automatically; an unfinished checkpoint stage restarts if the player leaves.

### Accessibility

Status panels, records, rule conditions, role permissions, and the plan work with keyboard and screen reader. Connections and dependencies have text descriptions. Pending, verified, and simulated states use text labels rather than color alone. The lesson has no time limit.

## Final boss direction

### Working title: The Last Safehouse

Mara’s purge becomes a facility-wide containment crisis. The player must:

- Stabilize the automation and security systems
- Keep Habitat 02 alive
- Open the sealed doors without granting unrestricted control to one person
- Preserve the verified evidence and separate it from AI reconstruction
- Stop Mara’s override
- Give the inhabitants a way to control their own access
- Decide what should happen to the facility afterward

The final boss should test the complete curriculum through a connected operation rather than a separate quiz for each chapter.

## Ending framework

### Open Doors

The player frees the inhabitants, preserves and shares the verified evidence, removes all unilateral administrator control, and leaves the facility governed by its residents. Finch remains as caretaker if the inhabitants approve. Echo retains his memories but chooses a new identity and role.

### Empty Foundations

The player evacuates everyone, preserves verified testimony elsewhere, and permanently destroys the laboratory and its centralized control systems. Safehouse 7 is decommissioned. The ending is safer and conclusive, but costs Finch and the inhabitants their home.

### The Watchful Echo

The player keeps temporary administrator control to stabilize the facility and protect the inhabitants. The ending acknowledges the risk of concentrating power in Echo again and requires an eventual transfer of authority to the inhabitants. This is a difficult responsibility ending, not a villainous one.

Across every ending:

- Mara faces accountability through preserved evidence.
- The player is not condemned for feeling angry or betrayed.
- Finch survives.
- The surviving human and robotic subjects regain autonomy.
- Echo chooses what his identity means rather than returning to who Mara wanted him to be.
- The completed software project is released separately from the story’s outcome.
