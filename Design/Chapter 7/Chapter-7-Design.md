## Lesson 1: Digital Literacy and Evaluating Information

**Depth:** Detailed.
**Workspace:** Split-screen Learn & Try.

### Story opening

Finch's temporary hold has stopped the purge for now. As he prepares a report for the people inside the Habitats, he finds several versions of the recovered records: original log entries, Mara's explanations, and an automatically generated summary.

> **Finch:** “We have evidence. We also have several descriptions of what the evidence supposedly means.”
>
> **Player:** “Are they different?”
>
> **Finch:** “In some important places. Before we share anything, we should check what the sources actually say.”

Finch opens the **Information Review Board**, displaying authorized source records beside claims and summaries. The protected evidence store remains sealed; the board uses recovered copies already available to Finch.

### Learn & Try pages

The left pane explains each topic, and the right side keeps the same **Information Review Board** available throughout the lesson, so the player can open a source, compare it with a summary, and watch what the board marks. All records, claims, and summaries are simulated, and the protected evidence store remains sealed. The player can move back and review pages during the checkpoint.

Examples use a temporary practice summary. Moving between lesson pages preserves the player's work on it, while **Reset example** returns the demonstration to its starting state without changing the checkpoint. The checkpoint begins from its own controlled snapshot of the evidence summary.

#### Page 1: Digital literacy

Digital literacy includes finding, understanding, evaluating, and using digital information responsibly. A polished presentation or confident answer does not guarantee accuracy.

Opening the two practice messages shows each one's author, date, and source beside the claims it makes, and marks the gaps the board cannot fill.

#### Page 2: Identify the source

Ask who created a record, when, what it was intended to show, and whether it is an original or a copy. A source may be useful for one question but not another. Missing information about its origin should be noted rather than assumed.

The player inspects a log entry's source, timestamp, and copy status.

#### Page 3: Facts, claims, and uncertainty

An observation records what the available evidence shows. A claim is an explanation or assertion that may need further support. An unanswered question should remain unanswered until there is enough evidence.

The board sorts a demonstration set into **supported**, **not established**, and **needs more information**, and shows the reason for each label beside it.

#### Page 4: Check context

An excerpt can omit surrounding information, and records can appear to disagree because they describe different times or events. Compare dates, nearby entries, and the original source before drawing a conclusion.

Opening an excerpt places it beside its surrounding entries, and the entries it left out are highlighted in place.

#### Page 5: Integrity is not the same as truth

A matching checksum can show that a copy matches the expected data. A verified signature can help establish that a record was signed with a trusted key and has not changed since signing. Neither proves the author was correct or the claims are true.

The board shows a verification result beside the claims it does and does not support, leaving the unsupported ones marked as open questions.

#### Page 6: Evaluate AI-generated summaries

An AI summary can help organize information, but it can omit context, confuse sources, or state an unsupported interpretation as fact. Compare important statements with the underlying records. Keep originals separate from summaries and label generated or reconstructed content clearly.

The board marks an unsupported sentence and shows the source text that does not contain it.

#### Page 7: Recognize misleading presentation

Selective excerpts, missing dates, altered images, or emotionally charged wording can mislead. Not every mistake is intentional; check the source and context before repeating a claim.

The player examines a fictional excerpt whose surrounding record changes its meaning.

#### Page 8: Share only what is needed

Before sharing a record, consider who needs it, what they need to understand, and whether restricted details should be withheld or redacted. Preserve the original separately; do not silently alter it.

The player creates a limited test excerpt for an authorized audience.

### Checkpoint: Review the evidence summary

#### Guided stage

Finch shows the player a preserved change record:

```text
AUDIT EXPORT SERVICE: DISABLED
CHANGE SOURCE: M.VALE
LABEL: TEMPORARY MAINTENANCE
```

A sample summary says: “Mara personally disabled the service to conceal evidence.”

The player identifies that the record names the **M.VALE account** as the change source, but this entry alone does not establish who used it or why. Finch helps revise the sentence without erasing the original record or presenting Mara's explanation as verified.

#### Main task

The player reviews a simulated evidence summary and its linked sources. The requirement is a reviewed summary in which every statement is supported, marked uncertain, or flagged as unverified, with its sources preserved and a safe excerpt prepared.

1. Inspect each source's origin, time, and whether it is an original or a copy.
2. Compare the summary with full source entries and their surrounding context.
3. Mark statements as **supported**, **not established**, or **needs more information**, considering all relevant sources.
4. Identify an AI-generated statement that goes beyond its sources and replace it with a supported description or explicit uncertainty.
5. Identify what a checksum or signature result does—and does not—verify.
6. Prepare a limited excerpt for an authorized audience without exposing unrelated restricted information. Preserve the source and label the generated summary separately.

The board gives specific feedback and allows revision. For example, repeating a summary's claim without checking its source returns the player to the source view, a statement that assumes who used an account is marked unverified, a checksum accepted as proof that a claim is true is returned to the evidence question, and a share attempt that exposes restricted material is blocked with an explanation. It does not ask the player to decide that all claims are true simply because integrity checks passed.

### Feedback and hints

Three hints point toward checking the original source, separating what a record says from an explanation, and reviewing the intended audience. They do not reveal the whole solution. Hints and retries are unlimited and penalty-free.

### Completion and story result

Finch keeps the reviewed summary alongside—not in place of—the preserved sources. Unsupported statements remain marked as unverified, and original records remain unchanged.

> **Finch:** “The evidence is strong enough to matter. That is all the more reason to say exactly what it shows.”

The reviewed material can support later communication and rescue decisions. Finch's temporary hold remains active; the inhabitants and their restricted records are not exposed by completing the lesson.

Checkpoint progress saves after each completed stage. If the player leaves, the current stage resumes with completed answers and prior work preserved.

### Accessibility

Source cards, comparison views, classifications, and redaction controls work with keyboard and screen reader, without drag-only actions. Focus moves in a predictable order from each claim to its linked source and context, and every control has a descriptive label. Labels, classifications, and feedback are announced as text and never rely on color alone, new results are announced when they appear, and the source comparison stays readable at large text sizes. The player can review the information without a time limit.

## Lesson 2: Technical Communication for Different Audiences

**Depth:** Detailed.
**Workspace:** Split-screen Learn & Try.

### Story opening

Finch's temporary hold is keeping the purge from proceeding, but the inhabitants need to know what is happening. The people maintaining the core also need enough technical detail to keep the hold stable.

> **Finch:** “The same situation needs two explanations.”
>
> **Player:** “One for the people inside, and one for whoever can help us keep the doors safe.”
>
> **Finch:** “Exactly. Neither needs exaggeration. Both need the truth.”

Finch opens the **Communication Desk**. It contains a simulated incident summary, reviewed evidence from Lesson 1, audience cards, and message drafts. Any urgent safety notice can be sent immediately; the exercise does not require the player to complete a report before warning someone in danger. All messages and recipients are fictional.

### Learn & Try pages

The left pane explains each topic, and the right side keeps the same **Communication Desk** available throughout the lesson, so the player can open a recipient card, draft on the practice incident, preview what an audience sees, and watch the status change. All messages, recipients, and deliveries are simulated. The player can move back and review pages during the checkpoint.

Examples use a temporary practice incident. Moving between lesson pages preserves the player's drafts on it, while **Reset example** returns the demonstration to its starting state without changing the checkpoint. The checkpoint begins from its own controlled snapshot of the containment messages.

#### Page 1: Start with the audience and purpose

Different people need different information to act. A technical teammate may need the affected system, status, and relevant checks. An inhabitant may first need to know whether a door is safe, what is being done, and how to ask for help. Neither audience needs unrelated private details.

The desk places two practice recipients side by side and shows what each one needs first, and what would be unnecessary for them.

#### Page 2: Be clear and specific

Use direct language, explain unfamiliar terms, and include the action or decision needed. Avoid unexplained abbreviations and vague instructions such as “fix it” or “everything is fine.”

The player revises an unclear line in the practice maintenance notice into a short, actionable update.

#### Page 3: Report the situation without exaggeration

Describe what is happening, its impact, and the urgency supported by the evidence. A temporary hold is not a permanent fix. Avoid promising that everyone is safe when that has not been verified.

Two draft alerts open side by side, and the desk marks the reassurance that the status panel does not support.

#### Page 4: Separate facts, uncertainty, and recommendations

Clearly distinguish what has been observed, what is still unknown, and what action is recommended. An explanation from Mara, an account name in a log, and a verified technical status do not all carry the same meaning.

A sample update opens with each sentence labelled as observation, uncertainty, or recommendation, and each factual claim linked to its source.

#### Page 5: Give a technical handoff

A useful handoff can include the affected system, current status, relevant symptoms, checks already completed, changes made, and the next requested action. Include timestamps or references where available. Do not send passwords, unrestricted evidence, or unnecessary personal information.

The player assembles a practice handoff for a fictional maintenance responder.

#### Page 6: Write for a nontechnical audience

Explain the immediate situation in everyday language. State what people can do, how they can ask for help, and when they can expect another update. If the answer is not yet known, say so. People affected should have an opportunity to ask questions and express their preferences.

The player rewrites the practice notice for a fictional building occupant without changing its meaning.

#### Page 7: Choose a safe channel and protect privacy

Use an approved channel for the intended audience. Share the minimum relevant information, and keep source records separate from any redacted copy or summary. A recipient's need for a safety update does not automatically authorize access to private records.

Each practice channel opens in review mode and shows exactly what its audience can see before anything is sent.

#### Page 8: Confirm understanding and follow up

A message being sent does not mean it was received or understood. Where possible, check delivery, invite questions, record the response, and provide updates when the situation changes. A lack of response should remain visible rather than being treated as consent.

The desk shows a delivery receipt and a recipient's question with the unanswered part marked, so the missing follow-up is visible rather than inferred.

### Checkpoint: Communicate the containment situation

#### Guided stage

Finch demonstrates the correction on a separate notice from earlier in the week:

> “Repair complete. All systems verified.”

The practice status panel shows one repaired service and two checks still open. The player changes the notice to say which service is repaired and which checks remain, and Finch points out that the problem was the missing scope, not the missing confidence.

The notice is not the containment message, and nothing from the demonstration carries into the main task.

#### Main task

The player prepares two simulated communications using the reviewed source material. The requirement is two messages, each accurate for its audience, each marking what is still unknown, each sent through an approved channel with a recorded delivery, and a reply that claims no more than the evidence.

1. **Technical handoff:** State that the containment request is temporarily held, identify the affected core and relevant status, summarize completed checks, and request help maintaining the hold. Mark unverified details as unknown and link relevant source records.
2. **Habitat update:** Explain the temporary hold in plain language, say that the team is working to open doors safely, and provide a way to ask for help or share preferences. Do not promise that the purge has been permanently stopped.
3. Select an approved fictional channel for each audience. Exclude credentials and unrelated restricted records, keeping the original evidence unchanged.
4. Review the messages, send them within the simulation, and check their delivery status. A simulated inhabitant asks a question; the player responds accurately, acknowledging any information still unknown.

The desk accepts clear equivalent wording rather than requiring one exact sentence. If a simulated message is undelivered, the player receives a visible status and can select an appropriate available channel or escalate; silence is not treated as consent.

### Feedback and hints

The desk highlights unsupported claims, unexplained technical terms for a nontechnical audience, unnecessary private details, and missing actions or follow-up, and the player can revise each draft. For example, promising that the purge is permanently stopped is returned to the status panel, sending credentials or whole records is blocked with an explanation, and a message with no recorded delivery stays visibly undelivered rather than being treated as sent.

Three hints focus on the recipient's needs, the difference between a temporary hold and a permanent fix, and what information is necessary to share. They do not reveal the whole solution. Hints and retries are unlimited and penalty-free.

### Completion and story result

The authorized recovery teammate receives the technical handoff. The Habitat update is delivered, and Finch records the simulated response and any remaining questions.

> **Inhabitant:** “You're really going to tell us what happens next?”
>
> **Player:** “What we know, what we don't, and what choices you have.”
>
> **Finch:** “And when the answer changes, we'll tell you that too.”

Finch preserves the messages separately from the evidence sources. The temporary hold remains active; sending an update does not open the Habitat or give anyone unrestricted control.

Checkpoint progress saves after each completed stage. If the player leaves, the current stage resumes with completed answers and prior work preserved.

### Accessibility

Focus moves in a predictable order from the recipient card to the draft, the preview, the delivery status, and the reply, and every field has a label that screen readers announce. Status, delivery, and feedback are announced as text and never rely on color alone; drafts can be read back in full and stay editable at large text sizes. There is no lesson time limit.

## Lesson 3: Collaboration, Escalation, and Responsibility

**Depth:** Detailed.
**Workspace:** Split-screen Learn & Try.

### Story opening

The Habitat has received Finch's update, and the recovery teammate has the technical handoff. But keeping the temporary hold stable, protecting the evidence, and planning a safe release require different responsibilities.

> **Finch:** “We have people who can help. We need to be clear about what each of us is doing.”
>
> **Player:** “And the people inside get a say in what happens to them.”
>
> **Finch:** “Yes. A rescue should not become another way to take control away.”

Finch opens the **Coordination Board**. It shows simulated roles, requests, current tasks, evidence-sharing controls, and a decision log. The temporary purge hold remains active; the board does not open doors or change real systems.

### Learn & Try pages

The left pane explains each topic, and the right side keeps the same **Coordination Board** available throughout the lesson, so the player can open a role, preview what a recipient sees, follow an acknowledgement, and watch the decision log update. All roles, requests, and responses are simulated, and the board cannot open doors or change real systems. The player can move back and review pages during the checkpoint.

Examples use a temporary practice response. Moving between lesson pages preserves the player's work on it, while **Reset example** returns the demonstration to its starting state without changing the checkpoint. The checkpoint begins from its own controlled snapshot of the coordination tasks.

#### Page 1: Work together with clear roles

A role describes a responsibility, not unlimited authority. Clear assignments help prevent essential tasks from being missed or duplicated. People may contribute to more than one task when appropriate.

Opening the practice roster shows each role beside its assigned tasks and marks the tasks that no role has accepted.

#### Page 2: Include the people affected

Inhabitants can have different needs and preferences. Give them accessible information and a way to ask questions or express choices. A person's silence does not mean they consent, and one person should not automatically speak for everyone.

Two practice responses open separately, and the board marks the difference between one person's preference and a preference for everyone.

#### Page 3: Share information responsibly

Give each recipient the information needed for their role. An authorized technician may need system status without receiving private subject records. Preserve original evidence separately from redacted copies or summaries.

The practice preview shows what a technician and an inhabitant would each see, with the restricted details that neither needs marked as excluded.

#### Page 4: Know when to escalate

Escalation means seeking help or a decision from an appropriate person or team when a problem exceeds your authority, expertise, or available information. Escalating is not abandoning the task: continue the safe work you are authorized and able to do.

The practice warning opens with three possible recipients, and the board shows which one has the authority and skills to answer it while the hold stays monitored.

#### Page 5: Challenge an unsafe instruction

When an instruction risks people, privacy, or evidence, pause the unsafe action, explain the concern, and seek an appropriate review. An instruction can come from someone with authority and still need to be challenged. In this story, the containment protocol is a facility safety system older than the network and runs under its own service identity, so Mara can request it as the facility's lead without any administrator rights; the player cannot assume a permissions check will block it.

A practice instruction to send a whole record “to save time” opens beside the restricted fields it would expose, and the board shows the safe alternative without carrying out either.

#### Page 6: Record decisions and disagreements

A useful decision log records the question, available evidence, responsible role, action, result, and any unresolved disagreement. Record what someone said separately from what has been verified. Documentation should not expose private details unnecessarily.

A practice decision opens in the log with one recorded disagreement beside it, and the original evidence stays visibly unchanged.

#### Page 7: Confirm handoffs and follow up

Assigning a task is not the same as confirming it was accepted or completed. Track who has acknowledged a request, what remains pending, and when to follow up. If no one responds, escalate using an available appropriate channel rather than assuming success.

A practice handoff shows one acknowledged task and one still pending, with the missing follow-up marked rather than assumed.

### Checkpoint: Coordinate the response

#### Guided stage

Finch demonstrates the challenge on a separate request from earlier in the week: skip the independent reviewer so a repair can finish sooner. The player identifies what the shortcut would bypass, declines it, and requests review, and Finch points out that the safe work could continue while the question was escalated.

#### Main task

The player uses the Coordination Board to coordinate one response. The requirement is every task assigned and acknowledged, consent recorded per person, sharing limited to each role, the warning escalated and tracked to a result, the unsafe instruction challenged and recorded, and the decision log holding the disagreement and the open items.

1. Assign monitoring of the temporary hold to the authorized recovery role and confirm the task is acknowledged.
2. Arrange an accessible channel for Habitat inhabitants to ask questions and share individual preferences. Record responses separately; an unanswered request is not consent.
3. Share a limited technical status with the recovery teammate and preserve the original evidence and any redacted copy separately.
4. Escalate a simulated life-support warning to an appropriate qualified responder while continuing authorized monitoring. The warning remains visibly **pending** until the responder acknowledges and reports a result.
5. Respond to an unsafe instruction to remove the evidence copy by explaining the concern and requesting review, rather than deleting it or assuming Mara's authority will be denied automatically.
6. Record the assignments, acknowledgements, evidence used, disagreement, and unresolved items in the decision log.

The board accepts equivalent safe assignments where responsibilities and authority are clear. It does not require the player to invent an inhabitant's preference or treat an unacknowledged handoff as complete.

### Feedback and hints

The board identifies unassigned work, information shared beyond a recipient's needs, pending handoffs, and unsupported assumptions about consent or authority, and the player can revise choices. For example, an unacknowledged handoff stays pending rather than becoming complete, sharing a whole record with a responder is blocked with an explanation, treating one inhabitant's reply as everyone's is returned to the individual responses, and assuming the platform will refuse Mara's request is corrected by the protocol's own record.

Three hints point toward matching responsibilities, checking acknowledgements, and continuing safe work while escalating. They do not reveal the whole solution. Hints and retries are unlimited and penalty-free.

### Completion and story result

Checkpoint progress saves after each completed stage. If the player leaves, the current stage resumes with completed answers and prior work preserved.

## Lesson 4: Capstone Decision-Making

**Depth:** Detailed.
**Workspace:** Split-screen Learn & Try.

>
> **Player:** “Then we check the systems, listen to them, and make sure no one can take control of everything again.”
>
> **Finch:** “A sensible plan. Let's test it before we put anyone at risk.”

### Learn & Try pages

All systems, checks, and plans are simulated, and testing never operates a real Habitat door or alters the preserved evidence. The player can move back and review pages during the checkpoint.

Examples use a temporary practice plan. Moving between lesson pages preserves the player's work on it, while **Reset example** returns the demonstration to its starting state without changing the checkpoint. The checkpoint begins from its own controlled snapshot of the Habitat 02 plan.

#### Page 1: Start with people and the immediate risk

Identify who could be affected, what is urgent, and what is not yet known. A temporary hold provides time to plan, but it does not resolve the purge. Ask inhabitants what they need and preserve different responses separately; silence is not consent.

The player reviews the current safety update and two fictional inhabitant responses.

#### Page 2: Check the systems before acting

A status indicator is a useful starting point, not always the full picture. Review the Habitat's life-support status, recent alerts, and available independent checks. Escalate a warning to a qualified responder rather than assuming a green indicator means every condition is safe.

The player compares a current status panel with a recent alert and a responder's simulated check.

#### Page 3: Apply the troubleshooting method

Not a new method. Chapter 6's loop — identify the symptom, gather evidence, form a theory, test one change, then verify the result — is the method here too, and the lesson does not restate it.

What changes is what is at stake while it runs. People are inside the systems being tested, so a change that would be merely inconvenient in a lab can be unsafe here, and a test that disturbs a Habitat's status can delay its release. Use the same loop and choose the least disruptive test first.

The practice fault opens with the last test results and the settings each option would change, so the single safe next step is visible before anything runs.

#### Page 4: Apply the network checks

The checks are the Chapter 6 set — loopback, the approved gateway, name resolution, and the tunnel — applied to a Habitat's reporting path instead of to the archive. A passing local test says nothing about the remote service, and a timeout alone does not establish why a test failed.

What is new is the consequence. If the communication path is down, the team cannot confirm a safe route before opening a door, so the release waits rather than proceeding on a green light elsewhere.

A practice Habitat's channel opens with its test message, its delivery state, and the acknowledgement that is still missing.

#### Page 5: Find the right records

A database can help locate the status and history needed for a decision. Select only relevant fields and use authorized, read-only access when changing data is unnecessary. A record's account name identifies the account recorded, not necessarily the person who used it.

A practice status table opens already filtered by Habitat, with the separate current check beside it and any difference marked.

#### Page 6: Review a proposed automation rule

A rule tells a system what to do when specified conditions are met. Conditions must reflect the intended safety checks, and an automatic action should not replace required human review or the inhabitants' choices.

The player inspects a simple simulated rule that requests a release review only after the hold, life-support check, and communications check are confirmed. The rule does **not** open a door automatically.

#### Page 7: Preserve backups and evidence

Verify that recoverable copies are available before making a risky change. Keep originals separate from working copies and AI-generated interpretations. A restore test checks recoverability; it does not prove that the contents are true.

The three copies open side by side — protected original, authorized working copy, and backup destination — with a restore test already run against the backup.

#### Page 8: Limit authority and plan a handoff

Give each role the access needed for its task, not unrestricted control. Important decisions may require independent review or more than one authorized participant. Document the plan, identify who will carry out each step, include how inhabitants can communicate their choices, and name who will hold authority once the crisis is over. That last question is settled only in the final scene, with the people it affects.

A practice access plan shows each role's reach beside it and marks the role that holds both evidence and Habitat-door control.

## Story purpose for the lessons

Each lesson should help the player answer a practical question raised by the finale:

- Can the evidence be trusted?
- Can it be explained without manipulating anyone?
- Who needs to receive it?
- Who gets to decide what happens to Safehouse 7?
- How can Echo protect the inhabitants without taking away their autonomy?

## Final Boss: The Last Safehouse

**Status:** Complete working design. Mechanics remain subject to implementation and playtesting.
**Goal:** Use skills from across the game to stop Mara's purge, protect and free the inhabitants, and preserve the evidence.

This is one connected operation, not a separate quiz for each chapter. Each part gives the player something needed for the next.

### Opening

The team is ready to act on the plan from Lesson 4. The temporary purge hold becomes unstable, and Mara renews her containment request across all Habitats.

```text
TEMPORARY HOLD: UNSTABLE
CONTAINMENT REQUEST: ACTIVE
SCOPE: ALL HABITATS
HABITAT 02: OCCUPIED
RECOVERED EVIDENCE: PRESERVED
PROTECTED EVIDENCE STORE: SEALED
```

> **Mara:** “You don't know what they'll do if you let them out.”
>
> **Player:** “They should get to decide what happens to them.”
>
> **Finch:** “Then let's give them that chance—and make certain the systems keep them safe.”

An independent fallback keeps the hold active while the player restores the recovery workstation. Finch warns that the hold is temporary, not a permanent solution.

### Timer, help, and safety

A **35-minute containment window** begins after the opening and instructions, with calm warnings at 15 and 5 minutes. Its length is subject to playtesting.

If it expires, the simulated workspace switches to manual recovery. The hold and life support remain safe, completed work and evidence are preserved, and the player has unlimited time to finish. The perfect bonus is forfeited, but the standard reward remains available. There is no game over.

The timer pauses during story scenes, the pause menu, help panels, mandatory accessibility overlays, while the game is closed, and during the final ending decision. It continues while ordinary boss sticky notes are open.

**Hint** has three progressive levels and comes from Finch. Before it opens, a warning says that using help forfeits the perfect bonus; confirming opens it, and cancelling preserves eligibility.

**Hear Mara** replaces the old Ask Mara control. Earlier bosses offered her as help; here she is not an ally, so the control becomes a story channel. It plays her statement on the current situation, recorded under her name as a claim, and never changes settings, evidence, access, or timing. Using it does not forfeit the perfect bonus.

Ordinary mistakes, retries, failed tests, and allowed undos do not forfeit the bonus.

All equipment, files, accounts, credentials, communications, code, and network operations are simulated. The protected evidence store stays sealed. Unsafe destructive actions are blocked with an explanation.

### Boss interface

The **Facility Response Console** provides a text-accessible room map, current objective, timer, bonus status, Habitat and system statuses, and access to **Unresolved Records**. The player can review completed actions and move between available stations. Later controls unlock when their prerequisites are satisfied.

### Mara's countermoves

Mara cannot grant herself access, so she never attacks the systems directly. She works on the people and on the record instead. She acts three times, and none of her moves can be prevented by a permissions check or by withholding access.

1. **A claim, not a command (Stage 6).** She submits a life-support warning that would make the player's own review rule refuse a Habitat. The console accepts it because submitting a claim needs no special rights, and the door stays blocked until the claim is checked. The player must verify it against the independent status rather than assume it or dismiss it.
2. **A policy she still reviews (Stage 4).** The recovery teammate's access is granted under a policy Mara keeps. She narrows that policy, which withdraws the teammate's role mid-stage and halts the technical work until the player re-authorizes the same limited access through their own recovery permissions. Nothing is seized; the procedure is used.
3. **An offer (Stage 5, after the rule is tested).** She offers to explain who the player was before the memory loss, in exchange for opening the doors without further review. Listening is allowed and costs nothing. Agreeing changes only what she says afterward, because she cannot open a door and does not control the review rule.

The player could revoke her console access outright, but the console treats that as removing the record of her claims and refuses it as evidence loss. She stays able to speak and unable to act.

None of the three forfeits the perfect bonus, none is bypassed by administrator rights, and all three are written into the decision log whether the player resists them or not. They do not change what the ending actions are; they change what Mara can claim afterward.

The tone flags set earlier change how she says all of this, never what she does. If the player kept the Chapter 5 evidence questions open, her lines are clipped and she treats the player as an adversary; if the player put the gateway first, she sounds almost friendly while making the same three moves. If the player filed the Lesson 3 record openly, she refers to that record by name; if not, she talks around it. A sharp order to wipe the archive at the end of Chapter 6 carries the same sharpness into her opening line here. Every flag is recorded beside her claims and never changes access, timing, rewards, or the endings.

### Stage 1: Restore the recovery workstation

**Skills:** Chapter 1 hardware, files, and permissions; Chapter 6 troubleshooting.

The workstation cannot reach the recovery tools because a fictional external connection is unplugged. Finch shows the device status and a safe-to-handle simulated connector. The player checks the evidence, reconnects the correct component, and confirms that the workstation recognizes it.

The player then locates an approved recovery file in the in-game file browser. A separate trusted registry identifies the expected file and verification information. The player checks the file before opening it, creates an authorized working copy, and leaves the original and the recovered evidence unchanged.

**Result:** The workstation can open the facility's read-only device registry.

### Stage 2: Find the affected systems

**Skills:** Chapter 3 databases, relationships, and SQL; Chapter 7 evaluating information.

The registry contains fictional Habitat and device tables linked by Habitat ID. The player uses an authorized read-only query to find Habitat 02's controller, communications device, and life-support monitor. The player checks the returned IDs and statuses against the separate current status panel.

The player also requests the available status for the other Habitats. Missing or unreachable entries remain **unknown**, not automatically empty or safe. The game accepts equivalent read-only queries that return the required information; it does not permit editing the records.

**Result:** Finch marks the identified controller and its communication path on the facility map.

### Stage 3: Restore the communication path

**Skills:** Chapter 4 networking; Chapter 6 troubleshooting and network tests.

The workstation can reach the local network but not the identified recovery controller. The player:

1. Checks the adapter and the approved gateway information.
2. Tests the local gateway and the controller path, then reviews a simulated trace and configuration.
3. Identifies and corrects the mismatched gateway setting. A timeout alone is not treated as proof of the cause.
4. Retests the route and verifies that the **actual recovery service**, not merely a ping, responds.

Failed tests can be reversed and retried. The now-reachable service supplies the current control and life-support status needed for the release plan.

### Stage 4: Protect evidence and assign access

**Skills:** Chapters 1 and 5 permissions and security; Chapter 6 backups and recovery.

Before the team changes the containment system, the player confirms that the recovered original records are preserved, a working copy is available, and a separate backup can be restored. The player restores a test file to a separate location and checks it against the expected backup version.

The player assigns the recovery teammate the limited access needed to perform the technical recovery and gives an independent reviewer the access needed to review it. Neither receives unrestricted control over both evidence and Habitat access. Authentication uses fictional simulated credentials only.

The player prepares a limited evidence package for an appropriate fictional recipient, keeping source records separate from AI-generated summaries. A checksum or signature verifies the applicable integrity or authenticity check; it does not prove every claim is true.

Mara narrows the policy that granted the recovery teammate's access. The teammate's role is withdrawn mid-stage, and the technical work stops until the player re-authorizes the same limited access through their own recovery permissions. The interruption is recorded, nothing is lost, and no unrelated access is granted to work around it.

**Result:** The team has a verified recovery copy, the authorized roles needed to proceed, and a record of the access change.

### Stage 5: Repair and test the review rule

**Skills:** Chapter 2 programming, conditions, testing, and debugging; Chapter 7 decision-making.

The recovery station displays a simulated rule that requests a Habitat release review when the purge hold alone is active. The player corrects the rule so that a review is requested only when **the hold is active, the relevant life-support check is confirmed, and communications are available**.

The rule requests human review; it does not open a door. The player tests at least three examples:

- All three conditions confirmed → review requested.
- Life-support check unconfirmed → no review requested.
- Communications unavailable → no review requested.

The player also checks that a failed or incomplete test is not shown as a successful review. The game accepts equivalent correct logic. The repaired rule is tested in the simulation before the authorized team applies it.

Mara offers to explain who the player was before the memory loss, in exchange for opening the doors without further review. Listening is allowed and costs nothing; agreeing changes only what she says afterward, because she cannot open a door and does not control the review rule. The offer is written into the decision log either way.

**Result:** The team has a tested way to request a release review without automatically opening a Habitat.

### Stage 6: Stop the purge and coordinate the release

**Skills:** Chapters 5–7 security, troubleshooting, communication, and collaboration.

The containment protocol is older than the network and runs under its own service identity, so Mara's request needs no administrator rights and grants her none; the game does not pretend a permissions check will reject it. The player uses the approved simulated recovery procedure to isolate the **purge execution path**, while keeping the separate life-support and Habitat communication services online.

The console shows the proposed impact before the change. The authorized recovery teammate confirms the technical step, an independent reviewer checks it, and the player verifies that the purge is no longer executing or queued. Life support and communications are checked afterward, and the action is recorded.

Before the first door, Mara submits a life-support warning for Habitat 02 that would make the review rule refuse the release. The console accepts the claim, because submitting one needs no special rights, and the door stays blocked until the player checks the warning against the independent life-support status. The status contradicts the claim, the claim is marked unverified in the decision log, and the review proceeds. Dismissing the warning without checking it, or acting on it, both leave the Habitat pending.

For each Habitat, the team checks its status, resolves or escalates warnings, confirms a safe route with a qualified responder, and communicates with the people inside. Door controls become available only after the relevant checks and review are complete. Opening a door gives inhabitants the **choice** to leave or remain where safe; the player does not make one decision for everyone.

Individual requests and unanswered messages remain distinct. A Habitat that cannot yet be checked stays visibly pending with an assigned follow-up; it is not silently counted as cleared. The player verifies each door's actual state after an authorized release.

**Hear Mara** remains available through Stage 6 and the ending decision, without letting her restart the purge or erase evidence. Her final accountability is left for the ending sequence.

### Final verification

The team reviews the facility-wide state:

- The purge is stopped and is not queued to restart.
- Life support and communications remain operational, with warnings addressed or explicitly escalated.
- Habitat statuses and door results are recorded; no unverified Habitat is labeled safe or cleared.
- The original evidence is preserved, and the backup restore has been verified.
- The limited evidence package has a visible delivery status, and unresolved matters have assigned follow-up.

When the required checks pass, the boss completes **automatically**—there is no separate Submit button.

### Reward

Finch gives the player a physical thumb drive containing the **Release Agent build component**.

- Standard completion awards the standard Release Agent, which guides release preparation.
- A perfect finish awards the upgraded version, which helps plan releases for multiple platforms. The player receives one version, not both.
- The player adds it to the in-game project build on the laptop. It is not used as an assistant during gameplay.
- After the full campaign, the completed seven-agent app is intended to be offered as a real-world download, subject to future implementation, safety, and testing. This is separate from the story ending.

### Ending decision

The boss ends with an explicit decision. It is not a menu and not an automatic outcome: the player chooses an action, and the game shows what that action costs. The decision is untimed, and the Release Agent has already been awarded, so nothing about the ending affects the bonus.

#### The scene

1. **The crisis closes.** The purge is isolated, every Habitat has a recorded status, the evidence package has a recorded delivery, and Finch confirms that no further system action is required.
2. **The record is shown.** The console displays the decision log from Lesson 3, each Habitat's individual responses, and any Habitat that never answered. Responses stay individual; the game never presents them as one collective preference.
3. **Every Habitat is offered the chance to respond.** Before the actions unlock, the player sends the release notice through the channel from Lesson 3. If any Habitat has not answered, the console states plainly: **“Choosing now decides for people who have not been heard.”** That is a warning and a recorded fact, not a block.
4. **Mara is heard once, last.** She may say what she believes about the inhabitants and about the player's right to decide. Nothing she says changes the available actions, and she cannot act. Her statement is recorded under her name as a claim.
5. **Three actions, each with an impact preview.** The console shows what each action does to the facility, the inhabitants, and the evidence before the player commits, using the same preview pattern as the purge isolation in Stage 6.
6. **The player chooses, then says why.** A short statement to the inhabitants, written or selected from equivalent phrasing. It changes tone, not outcome.
7. **One confirmation, then final.** The choice can be reviewed until it is confirmed, and the decision point is saved so a player can close the game mid-deliberation and return.

#### The three actions

| Action | Ending | What it does | What it costs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hand over** | Open Doors | Distributes administrator authority to the inhabitants, keeps no override, and leaves the facility governed by its residents | The facility stays fragile, and the player keeps no way to intervene if it fails |
| **Tear down** | Empty Foundations | Evacuates everyone, preserves verified testimony elsewhere, and destroys the laboratory and its centralized control systems | Finch and the inhabitants lose their home, and some records are lost with the systems |
| **Hold on** | The Watchful Echo | Keeps administrator authority temporarily to stabilize the facility and protect the inhabitants | Repeats the shape of Mara's mistake; the epilogue must show the first use of that authority and when it will be given up |

All three actions are always available. None is treated as the correct ending, and none is gated behind a score.

#### How conduct shapes the ending

Earlier conduct does not change which actions exist; it changes what happens inside the chosen ending. The epilogue reads from facts the boss already tracks:

- How many Habitats answered, and whether the player sent the release notice before choosing.
- Whether access was distributed by role in Stage 4 and Lesson 4, or concentrated in one role.
- Whether the evidence package had a recorded delivery, and whether originals stayed separate from summaries.
- Whether warnings were escalated and acknowledged, or left pending.

For example:

- **Open Doors after every Habitat answered:** the residents govern, Finch stays only if they ask, and the handover is clean.
- **Open Doors with a Habitat still pending:** the handover still happens, but it is contested. Some inhabitants learn of the decision afterward, and the epilogue says so.
- **Empty Foundations after unanswered notices:** the evacuation is orderly but imposed, and the epilogue records that people were moved rather than consulted, without framing the player as a villain for choosing it.
- **The Watchful Echo in any state:** the epilogue shows the first exercise of retained authority and the specific condition or date for giving it up. If none was set, the ending says that plainly.

No ending is withheld, no conduct is scored, and no player is condemned for anger or for choosing the hardest option.

#### Shared coda

The same closing material follows every ending:

- Mara's accountability rests on the preserved evidence and the recorded claims, not on the player's verdict alone.
- Finch survives.
- The surviving human and robotic inhabitants regain autonomy.
- The player's own documentation remains part of the record that outlives the decision.
- The completed seven-agent app is released separately from the story's outcome.

#### Accessibility and saving

Each action is presented as text with its preview and its costs, operable by keyboard and screen reader without a timer or precise pointer movement. Confirming is a deliberate two-step action with clear labels, and the decision point is saved so the player can return to it.

### Saving and accessibility

Progress saves automatically after completed objectives and individual actions. Closing the game pauses the timer; reopening restores the current station, actions, Habitat and evidence statuses, remaining time, and perfect-bonus eligibility. Sticky notes are removed when the boss ends.

The map, workstation, query controls, rule editor, messages, and door controls work with keyboard and screen reader. States and warnings use text, not color alone. Captions and reduced-motion alternatives cover alarms and story scenes. Timer expiry is announced without interrupting input. Restricted records remain unavailable through accessibility output unless authorized.

## Ending framework

The three endings below are the epilogue outlines the decision scene selects between. Each is written from the conduct recorded during the boss and Lessons 1–4, so two players who choose the same action can see different details without a different outcome.

### Open Doors

The player frees the inhabitants, preserves and shares the verified evidence, removes all unilateral administrator control, and leaves the facility governed by its residents. Finch remains as caretaker if the inhabitants approve. Echo retains his memories but chooses a new identity and role. The epilogue must show who holds authority afterward and how the first decision under resident control is made.

### Empty Foundations

The player evacuates everyone, preserves verified testimony elsewhere, and permanently destroys the laboratory and its centralized control systems. Safehouse 7 is decommissioned. The ending is safer and conclusive, but costs Finch and the inhabitants their home. The epilogue must show where the inhabitants go and what record survives the destruction.

### The Watchful Echo

The player keeps temporary administrator control to stabilize the facility and protect the inhabitants. The ending acknowledges the risk of concentrating power in Echo again and requires an eventual transfer of authority to the inhabitants. This is a difficult responsibility ending, not a villainous one. The epilogue must show the first use of retained authority and the condition or date for handing it over.

Across every ending:

- Mara faces accountability through preserved evidence.
- The player is not condemned for feeling angry or betrayed.
- Finch survives.
- The surviving human and robotic subjects regain autonomy.
- Echo chooses what his identity means rather than returning to who Mara wanted him to be.
- The completed software project is released separately from the story’s outcome.
